# Being known

*Step 1 of a successful career. Before anyone can pick you, the people who decide have to know you exist and know what you can do. There are two ways in: the open door — an application, done well — and the network. Most careers use both; the balance shifts toward the network as the roles get more senior.*

## Why this comes first

*Nobody is picked who isn't first known. How you become known depends on the role.*

For entry and most mid-level roles, the door is open: a job is posted, people apply, the best application gets the interview. The application is a real route and a skill in its own right — most people get their first two or three jobs this way, and doing it well is worth learning properly.

For the roles that matter most — the team lead, the first analyst on a new product, the person brought in to fix something — the door is usually not open. When a hiring manager needs someone for a role like that, the first thing they do is ask: *who do we know?* A name comes up from their peers, their manager, the senior people on the team. That person gets a conversation, often before the role is written down. If an application process follows, it confirms a choice that has largely been made.

This is not corruption; it is risk management. Hiring is expensive and often wrong, and someone a trusted colleague has seen deliver is a safer bet than a stranger with a good CV. The consequence: **the open door gets you in; the network gets you the roles that matter.** You need both, and the balance shifts over a career.

> **The one thing to remember:** early on, apply well and build the network in parallel. Later, you want to be the name that comes up when someone asks "who do we know?"

## The two doors

*Six ways to become known. The first is the open door; the other five are the network. They combine.*

| Route | How it works | When it works best | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Application** | You apply; a recruiter screens; a manager reads; you interview. | Entry and mid-level roles; any role at a company where you know nobody; high-volume hiring. The default route for most people's first jobs, and a real one at every level when done well. | Targeting the right roles, a CV that reads as evidence, a work sample, and — where possible — a referral that turns the cold application warm. See [below](#the-open-door-applying-well). |
| **Visible work** | Something you made reaches people you have never met: a public repo, a written-up analysis, a talk, an internal tool others adopted. | At every level. The work speaks before you do, and it keeps speaking for years. It also strengthens every application you send. | Doing work in the open, and finishing things well enough to put your name on. |
| **Reputation that travels** | Someone who worked with you is asked "who do we know?" and says your name. | Mid-career onward. This is how most moves after the first two or three jobs actually happen. | The people you worked with having seen you deliver, tell the truth, and make them look good. You cannot build this quickly; you build it every day in your current job. |
| **Introduction** | Someone already inside the circle brings you in: a coffee, a meeting, a project. | For roles that are not posted, or where the field is crowded. An introduction transfers the introducer's credibility to you for one conversation. After that it is yours to keep or lose. | Having earned enough trust with the introducer that they will spend their credibility on you. |
| **Being useful first** | You help someone in the circle — or someone they trust — with something they needed, before there is anything in it for you. | For the roles that matter most. It creates the relationship and demonstrates the work at the same time, and it puts the other person in your debt in the mild way that makes people want to reciprocate. | Noticing what they need, having the ability to help, and asking for nothing. |

The routes combine. Visible work strengthens an application and earns an introduction; the introduction becomes a chance to be useful; being useful builds a reputation that travels. A cold application backed by visible work and a referral is a different thing from a cold application alone.

## The open door: applying well

*The application is a skill. Most people do it badly because nobody taught them, which means doing it well is an advantage.*

| Step | What to do | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | Apply to fewer roles, chosen because you can say specifically why you fit and what you would do in the first quarter. Ten targeted applications beat a hundred sprayed ones. | A targeted application reads as evidence of judgment — the first ability they are assessing. |
| **Make the CV read as evidence** | Every line an outcome, sized: "Found a tracking break that accounted for half of a reported 8% conversion drop; fix recovered ~€X/week." Not "responsible for analysis of conversion." | Hiring managers skim for proof that you have done the thing. Outcomes are proof; responsibilities are not. |
| **Attach a work sample** | A public repo, a written-up analysis, a short memo — something they can open and judge in five minutes. A repo like this one counts. | It turns a paper candidate into a seen one. It is the application's version of visible work. |
| **Warm it if you can** | Before applying, find one person at the company — a second-degree contact, an alumnus, someone who posted about the team — and ask a specific question about the role. Then ask whether they would refer you. | A referral moves your application from the pile of hundreds to the pile of five. Most companies pay referral bonuses, so people are willing. |
| **Write the cover note for the manager, not the recruiter** | Three short paragraphs: why this role specifically, the one or two things you have done that match it, what you would want to look at first. | The recruiter screens on keywords; the manager reads for fit and thinking. Write for the second reader. |
| **Prepare the interview as a showing, not a telling** | The interview is where steps 1 and 2 meet: it is where you demonstrate the abilities, the traits, and the difference you have made. See the [interview guide](../interview/). | Everything in this repo has an *Interview lens* for this reason. |
| **Follow up, briefly** | A short note after each conversation with one thing you thought about afterwards. | It is follow-through, shown rather than claimed. |

> **The one thing to remember:** an application is not a weaker route; an *unprepared* application is. Targeted, evidenced, with a work sample and a referral, it is how most careers start — and it keeps working at every level.

## The network: the mechanism, piece by piece

*How influence is built in a circle you are not yet part of. Each step is small; the sequence is what works.*

| Step | What you do | What it looks like for an analyst |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Map the circle** | Find out who actually decides, and who they listen to. The org chart tells you the first; only observation tells you the second. | Who does the VP ask before a decision? Which PM's opinion carries weight with the head of product? Whose analysis gets quoted in the quarterly review? Write the names down. |
| **2. Find the edge** | Identify one person in or near the circle you can reach — a peer of theirs, someone who reports to them, someone who used to work with them. | The senior analyst who presents to leadership. The PM on the team whose work the VP watches. Someone you already know who knows them. |
| **3. Be useful to that person** | Help them with something they need, well, without being asked for anything in return. | Review their analysis before it goes up. Answer the SQL question in the channel. Send them the finding from your work that affects their area. |
| **4. Earn the introduction** | Once they trust your work, ask — or let them offer — to bring you into a conversation that matters. | "Could I join the metrics review?" "Would you introduce me to X — I have something on their funnel they'd want to see." |
| **5. Make the first contact about them** | In the first conversation with the decision-maker, bring something they need. Ask for nothing. | A sized finding in their area. A question they are wrestling with, answered. A clear, short view on something they are deciding. |
| **6. Repeat, in small pieces** | Build the relationship through many small contacts over months, each one useful, none of them an ask. | A follow-up when the thing you flagged resolves. A note when you see something relevant. Showing up to the meeting you were invited to, prepared. |
| **7. Let the opportunity be offered** | When a role or project comes up, you want to be asked, not to apply. If you have done 1–6, you usually will be. | "We're setting up a team for X — would you want to lead the analytics side?" By the time this is said, the decision was made weeks ago. |

Two things make the sequence work. **Reciprocity:** people want to repay help, and the repayment is often access. **Repeated exposure:** trust is built by frequency more than by intensity; six short useful contacts beat one long impressive one.

> **The one thing to remember:** you are not networking. You are being useful, repeatedly, to people one step closer to the decision than you are. The network is the by-product — and it is what turns your next application from cold to warm.

## In your first 90 days with a new circle

*A concrete version — joining a company, a new team, or a new stakeholder group.*

- **Weeks 1–2:** map. Who decides, who do they listen to, where do decisions get made (which meeting, which document). Ask your manager directly: "Whose opinion matters most on X?"
- **Weeks 3–4:** find the edge. Pick one or two people one step from the decision-maker. Learn what they are working on and what is hard about it.
- **Weeks 5–8:** be useful. One real contribution to each — a review, a finding, a question answered. Do it well. Ask for nothing.
- **Weeks 9–12:** the first contact. Through an introduction or a meeting you have earned a seat in, put one useful thing in front of the decision-maker. Short, sized, relevant to what they are deciding now.
- **After:** repeat in small pieces. A useful contact every two or three weeks. Keep a note of what each person cares about; mention it when it is relevant.

## The mistakes

*How this goes wrong, and how to tell when you are doing it.*

| Mistake | What it looks like | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Asking too early** | Requesting the introduction, the meeting, the role before you have been useful. | You have given them no reason to spend their credibility on you. |
| **Pitching instead of helping** | Every contact is about you — your skills, your ideas, what you want. | People avoid those who take; they seek those who give. |
| **Going straight to the top** | Cold-messaging the VP. | The VP's filter is the circle. Skipping it signals you don't understand how things work. |
| **Treating it as transactional** | Keeping score; expecting each favour to be returned. | Reciprocity works because it is not enforced. The moment it is, it stops. |
| **Only talking to your own kind** | Building relationships only with other analysts. | Opportunities come through *weak ties* — people in other circles who know things you don't. Your closest colleagues know what you know. |
| **Being known for the wrong thing** | Visible, but for volume, opinions, or self-promotion rather than for work that held up. | Being known is only useful if what you are known for is being right and being useful. |
| **Expecting it to be fast** | Giving up after a month. | This is a two-to-three-year process for a circle that matters. It compounds; most of the return comes late. |
| **Neglecting the open door** | Waiting to be found while not applying. | The network takes years; applications work now. Early in a career, do both. |

## Where this comes from

*The ideas under this page, if you want the sources.*

- **Weak ties** (Granovetter): most people find jobs through acquaintances, not close friends — because acquaintances sit in circles you don't, and know things you don't.
- **Structural holes** (Burt): people who connect otherwise separate groups get information and opportunities first. Being the analyst who talks to product, finance, and engineering is a structural advantage, not just a collaboration habit.
- **Reciprocity** (Cialdini; Grant's *Give and Take*): helping first creates a pull to reciprocate; consistent givers outperform takers over a career, provided they are not doormats.
- **Mere exposure and the frequency of contact**: trust and liking grow with repeated low-stakes contact. Six short useful exchanges beat one long impressive one.
- **Career capital** (Newport): rare and valuable skills are what you trade for opportunity — which is why none of this works if the work behind it isn't good. See [career capital](career-capital.md).
