# Career traits

*Step 2 of a successful career. Once you are known, you have to be picked — and what you are picked on, beyond the work itself, is a short list of traits. This page names them, from the inside: what each one feels like to have, what it feels like when it is missing, and how to grow it.*

## Why a structure

*So the list is complete, not just plausible.*

Lists of "traits for success" are usually someone's favourites. This one is built on the **Big Five** — the only model of personality with decades of evidence linking traits to career outcomes, and complete by construction: every trait anyone has measured loads onto one of its five dimensions. Two additions come from the models large companies use to identify high-potential people: **self-awareness** (from learning agility) and **grit** (sustained effort toward one direction over years).

The traits are described here from your side, not an employer's: not "what companies select for" but "what you need to have or build, and how you'll know it is missing." The same traits that get you picked are the ones you can develop.

These are different from the [dispositions](dispositions.md), which are about how you do the *work* — curiosity, scepticism, pragmatism. These are about what carries you *forward*.

> **The one thing to remember:** the Big Five says who you are; the career traits are the facets of that which decide who gets the chance.

## The seven traits

| Trait | Big Five root | What it looks like when you have it | What it feels like when it is missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Follow-through** | Conscientiousness | You deliver every time, not most times. The same person on a bad day. You stay with a slow problem or a slow relationship past the point others quit. | Strong starts that fade. Promises that mostly hold. A reputation for brilliance that nobody quite relies on. |
| **Ambition** | Conscientiousness (achievement) + Extraversion | You want more than the current role, you can say what, and you take the step toward it without waiting to be invited. | Waiting to be noticed. Watching less able people move past you because they asked. |
| **Courage** | Extraversion (assertiveness) + Emotional stability | You speak in the room, disagree with someone senior when the evidence says so, take the visible risk, say the number nobody wants to hear. | Having the right answer and not saying it. Agreeing in the meeting and complaining after. |
| **Composure** | Emotional stability | Pushback doesn't land as an attack. You recover from a public mistake in a day, not a month. You are steady when the quarter is bad. | Defensiveness when challenged. A mistake that becomes an identity. Pressure that leaks onto the people around you. |
| **Generosity with a spine** | Agreeableness, kept in balance | You help before you ask, make others look good, share credit — and you can still say no and hold a disagreement. | Either side alone: the helper who can't push back, or the pusher nobody wants to help. |
| **Structured curiosity** | Openness | You find complexity interesting rather than threatening, and you make it legible for others — the person who turns a messy meeting into three options. | Avoiding the unfamiliar problem. Or diving into it and leaving everyone else behind. |
| **Self-awareness** | Learning agility (cross-cutting) | You know how you land on others, where your blind spots are, and you seek feedback and use it. | Being surprised by your performance review. The same feedback, three years running. |

## Each one, overdone

*Every trait has a failure mode at the far end. Knowing yours is most of self-awareness.*

| Trait | Overdone becomes | The tell |
|---|---|---|
| Follow-through | Rigidity; not knowing when to stop | Finishing things that should have been killed. |
| Ambition | Politicking; self-promotion over substance | People start to check your numbers. |
| Courage | Recklessness; contrarianism as identity | Disagreeing to be seen disagreeing. |
| Composure | Detachment; not caring enough | Steady because nothing matters to you. |
| Generosity | Doormat; no boundaries | Your own work slips because of everyone else's. |
| Structured curiosity | Analysis as avoidance; frameworks for everything | A model where a conversation would do. |
| Self-awareness | Paralysis; endless self-examination | Knowing your blind spots and still not moving. |

> **The one thing to remember:** the goal is not the maximum of each trait. It is enough of each, in balance, with your own overdone version known to you.

## How they are tested in selection

*You are rarely asked about a trait directly. It is read from how you answer, what stories you choose, and how you behave in the room.*

| Trait | Where it is read |
|---|---|
| Follow-through | The outcomes in your stories. Whether you mention what happened *after* delivery. References. |
| Ambition | "Where do you want to be in three years?" — and whether the answer is specific. Whether you ask about scope, not just salary. |
| Courage | "Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone senior." Whether you push back on the interviewer's premise when it is wrong. |
| Composure | How you take a hard follow-up question. What you do when you don't know. The "tell me about a failure" question — is it a story or a wound? |
| Generosity with a spine | How you talk about former colleagues and managers. Whether your stories have other people in them. Whether you can say what you would *not* do. |
| Structured curiosity | Every case question. Whether you structure before you dive. Whether you make the interviewer's job easier. |
| Self-awareness | "What would your last manager say you need to work on?" — and whether you have an answer that costs you something. |

The [story bank](../interview/behavioural-story-bank-template.md) should carry one story per trait as well as per ability.

## How to grow them

*Traits move slowly, but they move. Each one has a practice that makes the behaviour cheap until it becomes the default.*

- **Follow-through** — build systems, not willpower: a decision log, a two-week follow-up on everything you deliver, a public commitment with a date. Finish one thing before starting the next.
- **Ambition** — say what you want to one person who can affect it. Once a quarter. Specific: the project, the scope, the role.
- **Courage** — practise at low stakes: one disagreement per week in a meeting where the cost of being wrong is small. The muscle transfers.
- **Composure** — separate the critique from yourself with a ritual: write the feedback down before responding; sleep on anything that stings. Review your own mistakes in writing so they become data.
- **Generosity with a spine** — one unasked favour a week; one clear "no, because…" a week. Keep both counts.
- **Structured curiosity** — when a meeting gets messy, be the one who says "I think there are three options here" and names them. Do it badly at first.
- **Self-awareness** — ask three people, once a year: "What's one thing I do that gets in my own way?" Then do something visible about the most common answer.

## Where this comes from

- **The Big Five / OCEAN** (Costa & McCrae; Barrick & Mount's meta-analyses): conscientiousness predicts performance across nearly all jobs; openness predicts success in analytical and creative work; emotional stability predicts performance under pressure; extraversion predicts advancement into leadership more than quality of work — a warning as much as a finding.
- **Grit** (Duckworth): passion and perseverance for long-term goals; the research name for follow-through over years.
- **Learning agility** (Lombardo & Eichinger; Korn Ferry): mental, people, change, and results agility plus self-awareness — the structure large companies use to identify high-potential people. Self-awareness is the dimension most often cited in senior selection.
- **Give and Take** (Grant): givers finish both first and last; the difference is boundaries. The source of "generosity with a spine".
