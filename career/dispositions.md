# The person behind the abilities

*Seven dispositions that run under all the abilities. They are not skills you learn from a book; they are habits of mind you notice in yourself, and that hiring managers — whatever their rubric says — are actually looking for.*

**On this page:** [Why this page exists](#why-this-page-exists) · [The seven](#the-seven) · [How they show up in interviews](#how-they-show-up-in-interviews) · [How to grow them](#how-to-grow-them)

---

## Why this page exists

*Abilities say what you can do. Dispositions say how you tend to act when nobody has told you what to do.*

Two analysts with the same abilities perform very differently. One asks why once more than required; the other stops at the answer. One reports the caveat; the other hopes nobody notices. One ships a rough answer on Tuesday; the other polishes until the decision has been made without them. The difference is not skill. It is disposition.

Job postings describe these in words like *proactive*, *results-oriented*, *comfortable with ambiguity*, *thinks out of the box*. Interviewers probe them with every "tell me about a time". They are worth naming so you can see which ones you have and which ones you are faking.

> **The one thing to remember:** the abilities get you hired. The dispositions decide what happens after.

## The seven

| Disposition | What it looks like | Its opposite | Which abilities it feeds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity that goes one step further** | On seeing a number, asking "why?" once more than the task requires. Opening the dashboard nobody opens. Reading the support tickets. | Answering the question as asked and stopping. | [Finding the ask](finding-the-ask.md), [analytical reasoning](../competencies/analytical-reasoning.md) |
| **Scepticism, including of yourself** | Asking "what would have to be true for this to be wrong?" — hardest on the findings you like most. Predicting the number before you run the query. | Believing the result because it is what you hoped. | [Analytical reasoning](../competencies/analytical-reasoning.md), [technical craft](../competencies/technical-craft.md), [working with AI](../competencies/working-with-ai.md) |
| **Tolerance for ambiguity** | Making progress without a clear question, a clean dataset, or a defined deliverable — and imposing structure rather than waiting for it. Holding several open threads without dropping any. | Needing the brief to be complete before starting. | [Analytical reasoning](../competencies/analytical-reasoning.md), [ownership](../competencies/ownership-and-delivery.md) |
| **Bias to action and iteration** | Shipping a rough answer on Tuesday rather than a polished one next month. Treating every analysis as a draft that improves with feedback. Proposing the cheapest test. | Perfecting the analysis until the decision has been made without it. | [Ownership](../competencies/ownership-and-delivery.md), [opportunity sense](finding-the-ask.md) |
| **Intellectual honesty** | Reporting the null result, the caveat, and the mistake when nobody would have noticed. Saying "I don't know" with what you would need in order to know. | Rounding toward the answer the room wants. | [Communication](../competencies/communication.md), [collaboration](../competencies/collaboration.md) |
| **Pragmatism about depth** | Knowing when 80% is enough and when it isn't. Choosing the segmentation over the model when the segmentation answers the question. | Over-analysis — as real a failure as sloppiness, and more common among strong technical people. | [Analytical reasoning](../competencies/analytical-reasoning.md), [business acumen](../competencies/business-acumen.md) |
| **Working in the open** | Doing the work where others can see it: a shared thread, a reviewed query, a documented analysis, a public repo. Letting people watch the thinking, not only the result. | Finishing privately and announcing. | [Collaboration](../competencies/collaboration.md), [ownership](../competencies/ownership-and-delivery.md) — and the whole of [career capital](career-capital.md) |

## How they show up in interviews

*Nobody asks "are you curious?". They ask for a story and listen for the disposition in it.*

| They ask | They are listening for |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about an analysis nobody asked for." | Curiosity, bias to action |
| "Tell me about a time you were wrong." | Scepticism of self, intellectual honesty |
| "Tell me about a project with an unclear brief." | Tolerance for ambiguity |
| "Tell me about a time you had to deliver fast." | Bias to action, pragmatism about depth |
| "Tell me about a result nobody wanted to hear." | Intellectual honesty |
| "How do you decide when an analysis is done?" | Pragmatism about depth |
| "Walk me through how you'd approach this." (any case) | All of them, live — especially whether you say "I'd check" and "I don't know" |

A story bank tagged by disposition as well as by ability is the practical preparation. The [story bank template](../interview/behavioural-story-bank-template.md) has both.

## How to grow them

*Dispositions change slowly, through practice that makes the behaviour cheap.*

- **Curiosity** — one unasked question per analysis. Write it down even if you don't pursue it.
- **Scepticism** — predict before you query, every time. The habit does the rest.
- **Ambiguity** — take the badly specified asks on purpose. Write the scope agreement yourself; that is the whole skill.
- **Action** — set a time-box out loud and ship what you have when it ends.
- **Honesty** — volunteer one caveat per memo that nobody would have caught.
- **Pragmatism** — before each analysis, write what "good enough for this decision" means.
- **Openness** — move one piece of work a month from private to visible: a reviewed query, a documented analysis, a short internal post.

> **The one thing to remember:** each of these is a behaviour you can do tomorrow once. Doing it a hundred times is what turns it into a disposition.
