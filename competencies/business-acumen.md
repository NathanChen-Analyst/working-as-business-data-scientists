# Business acumen

*Knowing how the business makes money, what the team that is asking is for, what decision is really on the table, and what a result is worth in business terms.*

## Why it matters

*What this ability changes about how you work.*

Every ask comes from somewhere in the business, and every recommendation lands somewhere in it. Without this ability an analyst answers the question as asked, reports results in percentage points, and is surprised when nobody acts. With it, an analyst answers the question that was meant, sizes results in money and customers, and knows whose problem the finding solves.

> **The one thing to remember:** a business question and an analytical question are different things. This ability is the translation between them, in both directions.

## The quick read

*Four lines to write before you start any ask. Ten minutes, on your first day or whenever a request comes from a part of the business you don't know.*

| # | Write down | Fill-in template | The running example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **How the company makes money** | *We sell [what] to [whom]. The big cost is [cost]. The number the company lives by is [metric].* | We sell goods through our own checkout and keep the margin. The number is orders × order value × margin. |
| 2 | **What the team that is asking is for** | *The [team] exists to [do what]. This quarter it is measured on [metric].* | The growth team exists to turn visitors into orders. It is measured on checkout conversion. |
| 3 | **Whether the team's number moves the company's number** | *[Team metric] moves [company metric] because [why]. It does not when [case].* | Conversion moves orders, and orders move the company number. It does not help the company if conversion is raised by discounting, which lowers margin. |
| 4 | **Where the question sits** | Is the question *the goal*, *a cause behind the goal*, *a limit on the goal*, or *unrelated to it*? | "Why is conversion down 8%?" is a cause behind the goal. The goal question behind it: "can we win those orders back before quarter end?" |

> **The one thing to remember:** the question you are asked and the goal it serves are usually not the same thing. Line 4 is where you find out.

**If a line stays blank**

- *Line 1 blank* — you cannot size anything yet. Spend an hour with someone in finance.
- *Line 2 blank* — the team has no clear goal, or you have not found it. Ask: "What is this feeding into?"
- *Line 3 blank* — nobody has checked whether the team's number helps the company. That is worth more than the ask.
- *Line 4 says "unrelated"* — stop. Find out what the question is for before doing anything.

**In an interview**, "tell me about our business" is asking for lines 1 and 2, written from public information — the annual report, the pricing page, a couple of earnings calls. Writing them before the interview is also the fastest way to decide whether you want the job.

## The toolkit

*Frameworks are ways of organising a question, not answers. Each one below is listed with the question it answers and the line of the quick read it helps you write. Eight to twelve of these, well understood, are worth more than fifty names.*

**For line 1 — how the company makes money**

| Tool | The question it answers | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| Business model canvas | What are the parts of this business and how do they fit? | Your first week in a business you don't know. Stage 1. |
| Unit economics | What does one customer, order, or subscription earn and cost? | Writing line 1; sizing any finding in Stage 6. In the running example, the 4% drop is worth ~€X a week only once you know order value, margin, and volume. |
| Reading a P&L | Where does the money go, and which lines can this decision move? | Knowing which findings the CFO will care about. Stage 1 and 7. |

**For lines 2 and 3 — the team and its goal**

| Tool | The question it answers | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| OKRs and team charters | What has this team said it is trying to do? | Writing line 2. If the team has none, that is a finding. Stage 1. |
| Metric trees and the north-star metric | Which one number captures the value the company creates, and which levers move it? | Writing line 3: company number at the top, team numbers below, causes below that. Stage 1 and 3. |
| Funnels (AARRR) | Where do we lose people between first contact and loyal customer? | The usual shape of a growth team's metric tree. Stage 2 and 5. The running example is a funnel question. |

**For line 4 — placing the question**

| Tool | The question it answers | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| Decision-first framing | What decision does this question feed, and what evidence would change it? | If no answer would change what anyone does, the question is unrelated to any goal. Stage 1 and 2. |

**For going deeper — the market around the business**

| Tool | The question it answers | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| PESTEL | What outside forces — political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal — could explain or threaten this? | The "external causes" branch of an issue tree. Stage 2. A new payment rule in one country would be a PESTEL hypothesis for a conversion drop. |
| Porter's five forces | How much pricing power and margin can this business expect? | Pricing and strategy asks; why margins differ across product lines. Stage 1 and 7. |
| SWOT | What are we good and bad at, and what is coming? | As a structured conversation with a stakeholder, not as analysis. It tells you what *they* think matters. Stage 1. |

**For going deeper — the customers**

| Tool | The question it answers | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| Segmentation, targeting, positioning (STP) | Which customers are we for, and what do we say to them? | Deciding which segments matter before cutting the data in Stage 5. "Returning users on the new payment method" matters because returning users carry most of the margin. |
| Jobs to be done | What is the customer trying to get done when they use this? | Choosing metrics around the customer's progress rather than the product's features. Stage 3. |
| Lifetime value and acquisition cost (LTV / CAC) | Is a customer worth what it costs to get them? | Most growth decisions. Know the rough numbers without looking them up. Stage 6 and 7. |
| Retention and cohorts | Do customers stay, and is that changing? | The number that compounds. Stage 3 and 5. |

**For going deeper — pricing**

| Tool | The question it answers | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| Price elasticity and willingness to pay | How much does demand change if price changes? | Pricing asks. Elasticity from past price changes or experiments; willingness to pay from surveys (Van Westendorp) or conjoint analysis. Elasticity from observational data is a causal claim — treat it as one. Stage 5. |
| Promotions and their hangover | Did the promotion create demand or just move it? | Pull-forward, cannibalisation, margin erosion. A promotion can work on revenue and fail on margin. Stage 6. |

> **The one thing to remember:** a framework earns its place only if it helps you write one of the four lines or answer a question a stakeholder is actually asking.

## In practice

*The habits that make this ability routine.*

- **Write the four lines for every new ask.** Ten minutes, and the difference between answering the question and answering the goal.
- **Know the numbers cold.** Revenue, margin, customer count, order value, LTV, CAC, the top three costs — roughly, from memory. It lets you size a finding in the meeting instead of next week.
- **Read what the business reads.** The board deck, the quarterly review, the strategy doc. They tell you what the organisation thinks matters.
- **Size everything.** Percentage points are not a result. "4 points of conversion on €2m of weekly checkout value at 30% margin is about €24k of margin a week" is a result.
- **Notice what cannot be right.** If the analysis says a tiny segment drives half the revenue, business knowledge should fire before statistics do.
- **Spend time with the people who run things.** Sales, support, operations, finance. An hour a month with each teaches more than any dashboard.

## What "good" looks like by level

*The same ability, at three depths.*

| | Junior | Mid | Senior |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowing the business** | Knows the product; learning how money is made | Can write lines 1 and 2 for any team; reads the board deck | Knows the strategy, the limits, and the politics; is consulted on what to measure |
| **Placing the question** | Answers the question as asked | Places it against the goal; reframes when it is a cause or unrelated | Shapes which questions get asked at all |
| **Sizing** | Reports metrics | Sizes findings in money and customers | Sizes findings against the alternatives the business is weighing |
| **Frameworks** | Knows the names | Knows which question each answers; uses two or three fluently | Knows when none fit and builds the frame from the business itself |
| **Commercial judgment** | Flags surprising results | Catches results that cannot be right before they go out | Anticipates what the CFO, the CPO, and the CEO will each ask |

## Interview lens

*How this ability is tested, and what interviewers are listening for.*

| Question | What they want to hear |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about our business." | Line 1, and line 2 for the team you would join, from public information. Most candidates describe the product; strong ones describe how the money works. |
| "How would you measure the success of [feature]?" | Start from what the feature is for — the team's goal — then a primary metric that maps to it, guardrails, and what you would trade off. |
| "Our revenue is down 10%. Where would you look?" | A decomposition that follows how the business makes money — customers × orders per customer × value per order — before any data. |
| "Estimate the size of [market / opportunity]." | A structured estimate with stated assumptions. The reasoning is the answer. |
| "Should we run this promotion?" | Demand uplift against margin erosion and pull-forward, and what you would measure to know. |
| "What's a metric this company tracks that you think is misleading?" | Evidence that you think about what a metric is *for*. The hardest one to fake; the one that shows line 3. |

**Story bank prompts (STAR)**

1. A time you reframed an ask around the real goal behind it
2. A finding you sized in business terms, and what that changed
3. A result that looked right in the data and wrong against the business, and what you did
4. A time a team's number and the company's number pulled in different directions, and how you handled it
